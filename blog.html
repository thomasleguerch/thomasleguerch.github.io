Stochastic Integration and Stochastic Equations

I wish to emphasize a few points which I feel are little known

  • Brownian motion is critical with respect to ordinary calculus. Brownian-driven differential equations can almost be made sense of using Young integrals.
  • Stochastic calculus is a vague term referring to a set of tools (integral, derivative, chain rule) fit for the study of stochastic processes.
  • Stochastic calculus is not quite an extension of ordinary calculus. Rather than relying on the regularity of functions, it relies mostly on the probabilistic properties of stochastic processes.
  • Itô calculus is the classical stochastic calculus associated with semimartingales.
  • In the narrowest sense, stochastic differential equations are equations involving an Itô integral.
  • Stochastic differential equations based on Itô integrals may be used to model dynamical systems disturbed by noise. This is possible only when the noise is a semimartingale.

A pedagogical reordering of History

Stochastic differential equations are often presented as a natural extension of ordinary differential equations (ODE), see for instance {{Evans}}{{legall}}. The ODE $$\dot{x}_t=b(x_t)$$ can be upgraded to the following « ODE with noise » \[\dot{X}_t=b(X_t)+\sigma\xi_t\,,\] where \(\sigma\in\mathbb R\) and \(\xi\) is some random forcing. By random forcing, one could think of an irregular function, but we mean a stochastic process. This means that for every $t$, $\xi_t$ is a random variable which depends on a chance parameter $\omega$. Notice that $x$ became $X$, to emphasise that $X$ depends on $\omega$. The strength parameter \(\sigma\) can be further chosen to depend on \(X_t\): \begin{equation}\label{eq:ODE_with_noise} \dot{X}_t=b(X_t)+\sigma(X_t)\xi_t\,. \end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:ODE_with_noise} can be loosely described as a controlled differential equation with random control. In the context of physical or financial modelling, the random forcing \(\xi\) stems from unknown degrees of freedom, and it is quite natural to take \(\xi\) to be a Gaussian white noise (centred Gaussian impulses occurring randomly and equally at every frequency). In this context, typical realisations of \(\xi\) are generalised functions Random does not mean rough but most interesting random objects are rough., and \eqref{eq:ODE_with_noise} involves the product of a generalised function with a non-smooth function, which is an ill-defined operation. Equations of the type \begin{equation}\label{eq:ODE_controlled} \dot x_t=b(x_t)+\sigma(x_t)\dot g_t \end{equation} classically make sense for smooth enough controls ($g$ should be Lipschitz or BV), but white noise is not smooth enough. This means that, for typical realisations of Gaussian white noise, \eqref{eq:ODE_with_noise} cannot be interpreted in a classical pathwise sense.

Notice that, even though typical realisations of \(\xi\) are generalised functions, they are the derivative of a true function. In other words, there exists a random process \(B\) (whose typical realisations are true functions) such that, in the sense of generalised functions, \begin{equation*} \frac{\dd B}{\dd t}=\xi\,. \end{equation*} It is tempting to rewrite formally \eqref{eq:ODE_controlled} in integral form \begin{equation}\label{eq:proto_SDE_integral} x_t=x_0+\int_0^tb(x_s)\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(x_s)\,\dd g_s\,. \end{equation} Having in mind functions $g$ that are typical realisations of $B$, equation \eqref{eq:proto_SDE_integral} is equally ill-posed as \eqref{eq:ODE_with_noise} from an analytical point of view. This time, the issue is not about multiplying a distribution with a non-smooth function, it is about making sense of the integral w.r.t. to $g$. One could expect to rely on Stieltjes integrals, but one would need $g$ to be Lipschitz or BV. This analytical problem is akin to multiplying a distribution with a non-smooth function.

The authors of modern stochastic analysis textbooks usually remark — after Lévy and Itô — that typical realisations of $B$ are not Lipschitz or BV, and that tailor-made Itô integrals may be constructed and used.

How is this a slight reordering of History?

Stochastic differential equations are often introduced in the context of Itô calculus, which provides a natural framework for integration with respect to semimartingales. In this setting, the Itô integral and the associated calculus form a coherent toolkit for studying stochastic dynamics driven by Brownian motion, and more generally, semimartingales.

In many applications, especially in physics and finance, one is led to consider stochastic perturbations that go beyond this class. While semimartingales cover a large and highly relevant family of noises, they do not exhaust all models of interest. In particular, processes with long-range dependence or rougher structure fall outside the scope of classical Itô theory, and require alternative notions of integration.

As a result, «stochastic calculus» is often used in a broader sense than Itô calculus alone, even though the latter provides the central and most widely used framework.

Also, it could be argued that SDEs find their roots in the Langevin equation when the noise is multiplicative (multiplied by a function of the unkown, as in \eqref{eq:ODE_with_noise}). I am writing this post from a maths perspective, so I may be missing some background, but I assume that Itô was the first one to propose a consistent interpretation of \eqref{eq:ODE_with_noise}.

We will rely on {{IW}}{{KS}}{{RY}} and the seminal work {{Ito}} This memoir was written by Itô as an extension and as an English translation of an article written in Japanese in 1942. to recount a more accurate history. The advent of SDEs is to be found in the theory of Markov processes rather than in the theory of ODEs.

A more accurate history of SDEs

It is well known that one can associate a homogeneous Markov process with an operator called the infinitesimal generator which describes how the process moves from point to point. For instance, if \(B_t\) is an \(\mathbb R^d\)-valued Brownian motion, \(X_t=x+b t+\sigma B_t\) is a Markov process with generator given by \begin{equation}\label{eq:generator_arithmetic_BM} Lf(x)=\sum_ib_i\partial_if(x)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\partial_{ij}^2f(x),\quad f\in\mathcal C^2_0\,, \end{equation} where \(a=\sigma\sigma^t\). More generally, a large class of Markov process with continuous sample paths can be shown to have the following generator \begin{equation}\label{eq:generator_general} Lf(x)=\sum_ib_i(x)\partial_i f(x)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}a_{ij}(x)\partial_{ij}^2f(x),\quad f\in\mathcal C^2_ K\,, \end{equation} where the matrix \(a(x)\) is symmetric and non-negative (see {{RY}}, p. 267). These Markov processes are called diffusions.

The methodology of stochastic differential equations

Let us suppose that there exists a Markov process \(X\) with generator \eqref{eq:generator_general}. Taking the generator at face value suggests that between times \(t\) and \(t+h\), \(X\) satisfies \begin{equation*} X_{t+h}=X_t+b(X_t)h+\sigma(X_t)(B_{t+h}-B_t)+o(h)\,, \end{equation*} where \(\sigma\sigma^t=a\) and \(B_t\) is a Brownian motion. One may express the same idea in integral form \begin{equation}\label{eq:SDE} X_t=x+\int_0^tb(X_s)\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(X_s)\,\dd B_s\,. \end{equation} Notice how \eqref{eq:SDE} resembles \eqref{eq:proto_SDE_integral}, even though we obtained them in unrelated ways. The analytical problem we already mentioned still persists, but this time $B$ is naturally a Brownian motion.

This is how SDEs first appeared historically. People noticed that some Markov processes ought to satisfy stochastic differential equations. If one came up with a way of defining the (analytically challenging) second integral appearing in \eqref{eq:SDE}, then one could construct \(X\) as the solution to \eqref{eq:SDE}. According to {{IW}}{{KS}}, this approach to diffusions was proposed by P. Lévy and is dubbed in {{KS}} as the methodology of stochastic differential equations To be compared with the method of parabolic equations due to Kolmogorov, which relies on the parabolic partial differential equation \(\partial_tp=Lp\) to define transition probabilities. The Kolmogorov extension theorem then serves to obtain the existence of a corresponding Markov process, and lastly, the Kolmogorov continuity theorem allows to obtain a continuous modification.. The merit of defining the stochastic integral with respect to \(W\) rests on Itô and is presented in {{Ito}}.

Itô's stochastic integral

In modern parlanceItô did not mention martingales or adaptedness. He rather used that for every $s$, the family $\{H_r\,,\ 0\le r\le s\}$ is independent from the family $\{B_u-B_s\,,\ s\le u\le t\}$, Itô noticed that Riemann-type sums like \[\sum_iH_{t_i}(B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_i})\] converge in $L^2(\mathbb P)$ along sequences of partitions with vanishing mesh, provided $H$ is adapted to the filtration of $B$. This relies on the martingale property of $B$ and on the so-called Itô isometry:

Theorem (Itô isometry) For every simple predictable process $H$ that reads \begin{equation}\label{eq:simple_process} H_t=\sum_{i}H_i\mathbbm{1}_{]t_i,t_{i+1}]}(t)\,, \end{equation} denote the associated Riemann-type sum \[(H\cdot B)_t:=\sum_iH_i(B_{t_{i+1}\wedge t}-B_{t_i\wedge t})\,.\] Then, \begin{equation}\label{eq:ito_isometry} \mathbb E\left[(H\cdot B)_t^2\right]=\int_0^t\mathbb E[H_s^2]\,\dd s\,. \end{equation}

The Itô isometry \eqref{eq:ito_isometry} essentially means that a convenient Itô integral $(H\cdot B)_t$ exists for every adapted process $H$ such that $\int_0^t\mathbb E[H_s^2]\,\dd s<\infty$. This is done by approximating $H$ by processes of the type \eqref{eq:simple_process} and taking limits in the appropriate spaces. The limit is usually denoted \[\int_0^tH_s\,\dd B_s\,,\ \text{or}\ (H\cdot B)_t\,,\] and is called the Itô integral. The second notation is more pedagogical. It emphasises that the integral is not a usual integral.

Remark. At first, the notation \(\int_0^t H_s\,\mathrm dB_s\) is somewhat suggestive: the differential \(\mathrm dB\) has no intrinsic meaning yet, and no calculus rules have been established. In this sense, it is reminiscent of writing \(\sum X_n\) for a series that only converges in \(L^2(\mathbb P)\), where the notation may suggest a stronger mode of convergence than is actually available. The difference, however, is that the Itô integral is defined via an \(L^2\)-limit, so the mode of convergence is built into its construction.

The Itô isometry is one of the most important mathematical facts behind the Itô integral.

Back to diffusion processes — Itô's breakthrough: Itô calculus

We shall keep in mind that Itô's programme was to give substance to the so-called methodology of stochastic differential equations. Having defined a notion of integral with respect to $B$, and interpreting the problematic integral in \eqref{eq:SDE} as an Itô integral, there remained to prove that

  1. there exists a solution $X$ to \eqref{eq:SDE};
  2. $X$ has the Markov property;
  3. $X$ has $b$ (resp. $\sigma$) as its drift (resp. diffusion) coefficient.

Assuming that $b$ and $\sigma$ are Lipschitz continuous and have at most linear growth (similar conditions appear in the Picard-Lindelöf theorem), a unique solution $X$ can be proved to exist by a Banach fixed point argument.

Itô then derived a chain rule for his integral. Generically, the solution $X$ to \eqref{eq:SDE} will be a process of the kind \begin{equation}\label{eq:ito_process} X_t=x+\int_0^tb_s\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma_s\,\dd B_s\,. \end{equation} For such processes, called Itô processes, the chain rule reads for every $f\in\mathcal C^2$, \begin{equation}\label{eq:ito_formula} f(X_t)=f(x)+\int_0^tf'(X_s)\sigma_s\,\dd B_s+\int_0^t\underbrace{\left[\vphantom{\frac12}\right.f'(X_s)b_s+\overbrace{\frac12f''(X_s)\sigma^2_s}^{\text{Itô corr. term}}\left.\vphantom{\frac12}\right]}_{:=L_sf(X_s)}\,\dd s\\ \end{equation} Notice the appearance of a corrective term. Taking expectation in \eqref{eq:ito_formula} yields \begin{equation*} \mathbb E[f(X_t)]=\mathbb E[f(X_s)]+\int_s^t\mathbb E[L_uf(X_u)]\,\dd u\,, \end{equation*} or \begin{equation*} P_{s,t}f(x)=f(x)+\int_s^tP_{s,u}L_uf(x)\,\dd u\,. \end{equation*} Differentiating the latter equation with respect to $t$ yields the Kolmogorov forward equation $$ \partial_tP_{s,t}f(x)=P_{s,t}L_tf(x)\,. $$ This implies that $X$ is a Markov process with desired generator. In particular, it has the desired drift $b$ and diffusion coefficient $\sigma$.

Analytical trouble, Young integrals and the \(p\)-variation

This section is about attempts to make sense of integrals with respect to $B$ relying on real analysis. That is to say, we are looking for an integration theory which extends Stieltjes integration while being general enough to allow integration with respect to typical realisations of Brownian motion.

Bearing in mind that we wish to make sense of integrals of the type $$ \int_0^tf_s\,\dd g_s\,, $$ we naturally wish to examine the convergence of Riemann-type sums \begin{equation}\label{eq:riemann_sums} \sum_i f_{t_i}(g_{t_{i+1}}-g_{t_i}) \end{equation} when the mesh of the partition goes to $0$. One may argue that Riemann sums are too simplistic, especially because we know that modern integration theory is based on the Lebesgue integral. That is true, but $B$ is not BV ($\dd B$ is not a measure), so there is no hope to interpret stochastic integrals as Lebesgue integrals with respect to a random measure.

Let us introduce $p$-variation, which is of particular interest when it comes to giving criteria for the convergence of Riemann sums.
Definition We define the \(p\)-variation on the interval \(I\subset[0,\infty)\) of any function \(f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb R\) as the quantity \begin{equation*} \|f\|_{p-\text{var}}^I:=\sup_{D(I)}\left(\sum_{k}\left|f(t_{k+1})-f(t_k)\right|^p\right)^{1/p}\,, \end{equation*} where the supremum runs over all subdivisions of \(I\).

Remark \(2\)-variation is different from quadratic variation. For instance, $B$ famously has a finite quadratic variation, but we will soon see that its $2$-variation is infinite.

Here is the general criterion for the convergence of Riemann sums under $p$-variation hypotheses:

Theorem (Young-Kondurar) If $\|f\|_{p-\text{var}}^{[0,t]}<\infty$ and $\|g\|_{q-\text{var}}^{[0,t]}<\infty$ with $p^{-1}+q^{-1}>1$, then the Riemann-sums converge along any sequence of subvisions of $[0,t]$ with vanishing mesh, to the same limit. We call this unique limit the Young-Kondurar integral of $f$ w.r.t. $g$.

Remark Stieltjes integrals correspond to $q=1$ and any $p\ge1$.

The previous theorem is pretty significant, because the solution $x$ to \eqref{eq:proto_SDE_integral} is known to inherit its regularity from that of $g$. This means that, even assuming $\sigma$ to be smooth, we expect that $q=p$, and the criterion requires $p<2$. At this point, there is still hope that Young integrals are enoug to make sense of \eqref{eq:SDE}, but unfortunately:

Theorem For every \(p\le2\), for every interval \(I\subset[0,\infty)\), \(\|B\|_{p-\text{var}}^I=\infty\) a.s. In particular, a.s., \(B\) has infinite 2-variation over all intervals. The same result holds for any continuous semimartingale.

Quite often, courses on stochastic analysis insist that \(B\) has infinite variation (infinite \(1\)-variation) over all intervals, ruling out the possibility to use Stieltjes integrals to make sense of \eqref{eq:SDE}. The previous theorem rules out Young-Kondurar integrals altogether. This is a very significant analytical fact.

Remark En passant, we learned that controlled equations of the type \eqref{eq:ODE_controlled} can be made sense of by \eqref{eq:proto_SDE_integral} where the integral w.r.t. to $g$ is a Young integral, as long as $g$ has finite $p$-variation with $p<2$.

One might expect that $p$-variation is not the final answer, as there exist other ways to measure the regularity of functions, but the next theorem indicates that this approach is insufficient on its own.

Theorem (Lyons 91) There exists no separable Banach space $\mathcal B\subset\mathcal C([0,1])$ with the following properties

  • Samples paths of Brownian motion lie in $\mathcal B$ almost surely.
  • The map $(f,g)\mapsto\int_0^{\cdot} f_t\dot g_t\,\dd t$ defined on smooth functions extends to a continuous map from $\mathcal B\times\mathcal B$ into $\mathcal C([0,1])$.

Remark In particular, there exists no Banach topology on $\mathcal C([0,1])$ strong enough to keep the Sieltjes integral continuous in its arguments and weak enough to allow smooth paths to converge to Brownian paths.

On a theme of Wong, Zakai and Stratonovich

A natural way to interpret stochastic differential equations driven by Brownian motion is to approximate the driving noise by smooth processes (e.g. mollification or piecewise linear interpolation) and consider the corresponding classical differential equations. From a Physics perspective, this makes sense because white noise is an idealisation of rapidly varying smooth forces.

For $\varepsilon>0$, consider $\rho_\varepsilon$ a smooth function with compact support. Let $B$ a Brownian motion and define $B^\varepsilon_t:=(B\star\rho_\varepsilon)_t$. The paths of $B^\varepsilon$ are smooth functions, so the equation \[X^\varepsilon_t=x+\int_0^tb(X^\varepsilon_s)\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(X^\varepsilon_s)\,\dd B^\varepsilon_s\] can be interpreted as a usual ODE involving a Stieltjes integral.

It can be proved that $X^\varepsilon$ converges to a process $X$ in $\mathcal C([0,1])$ in probability as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. The limiting process $X$ does not satisfy a similar equation with a Stieltjes, or even a Young integral, as we already understood. However, $X$ satisfies a weaker equation where the integral is an Itô integral. Quite surprisingly, $X$ satisfies \[X_t=x+\int_0^tb(X_s)\,\dd s+\frac12\int_0^t\sigma'(X_s)\sigma(X_s)\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(X_s)\,\dd B_s\,.\] Notice the appearance of an extra term.
