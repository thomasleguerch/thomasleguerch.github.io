Stochastic integration and stochastic equations

What is a stochastic differential equation?

Stochastic integrals are not merely random Lebesgue integrals or Stieltjes integrals. They are fundamentally defined as martingales. SDEs are equations that involve stochastic integrals and whose purpose is to define or characterise some Markov processes called diffusion processes. SDEs may be used to make sense of ODEs with noise only when the integral of the noise is a semimartingale (the sum of a finite variation process and a martingale).

The mythology of stochastic differential equations

A pedagogical shortcut often taken to introduce stochastic differential equations is to present them as a natural extension of ordinary differential equations (ODE), see for instance \cite{Evans,legall}, p. 209. The ODE \[\dot{x}_t=b(x_t)\] can be upgraded to the following « ODE with noise » \[\dot{X}_t=b(X_t)+\sigma\xi_t\,,\] where \(\sigma\in\mathbb R\) and \(\xi\) is some random forcing. By random forcing, one could think of an irregular function, but we mean a stochastic process. This means that for every $t$, $\xi_t$ is a random variable which depends on a chance parameter $\omega$. Notice that $x$ became $X$, to emphasise that $X$ depends on $\omega$. The strength parameter \(\sigma\) can be further chosen to depend on \(X_t\): \begin{equation}\label{eq:ODE_with_noise} \dot{X}_t=b(X_t)+\sigma(X_t)\xi_t\,. \end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:ODE_with_noise} can be loosely described as a controlled differential equation with random control. In the context of physical or financial modelling, the random forcing \(\xi\) stems from unknown degrees of freedom, and it is quite natural to take \(\xi\) to be a Gaussian white noise (centred Gaussian impulses occurring randomly and equally at every frequency). In this context, typical realisations of \(\xi\) are generalised functions [0], and \eqref{eq:ODE_with_noise} involves the product of a generalised function with a non-smooth function, which is an ill-defined operation. Equations of the type \begin{equation}\label{eq:ODE_controlled} \dot x_t=b(x_t)+\sigma(x_t)\dot g_t \end{equation} classically make sense for smooth enough controls ($g$ should be Lipschitz or BV), but white noise is not smooth enough. This means that it is impossible to make sense of \eqref{eq:ODE_with_noise} for typical realisations of Gaussian white noise.

Notice that, even though typical realisations of \(\xi\) are generalised functions, they are the derivative of a true function. In other words, there exists a random process \(B\) (whose typical realisations are true functions) such that, in the sense of generalised functions, \begin{equation*} \frac{\dd B}{\dd t}=\xi\,. \end{equation*} It is tempting to rewrite formally \eqref{eq:ODE_controlled} in integral form \begin{equation}\label{eq:proto_SDE_integral} x_t=x_0+\int_0^tb(x_s)\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(x_s)\,\dd g_s\,. \end{equation} Having in mind functions $g$ that are typical realisations of $B$, equation \eqref{eq:proto_SDE_integral} is as problematic as \eqref{eq:ODE_with_noise} from an analytical point of view. This time, the issue is not about multiplying a distribution with a non-smooth function, it is about integrating a function with respect to another one without any guarantee on their regularity. Indeed, to make sense of the integral with respect to $g$, one could expect to rely on Stieltjes integrals, but one would need $g$ to be Lipschitz or BV. This analytical problem is akin to multiplying a distribution with a non-smooth function.

And yet, the authors of stochastic analysis textbooks rarely ponder on the analytical difficulty posed by making sense to \eqref{eq:proto_SDE_integral} for typical realisations of $B$. They remark that typical realisations of $B$ are not Lipschitz or BV, and usually pull Itô integrals out of the hat. At this point, they usually keep quiet about the fact that Itô integrals an only really useful when $B$ is a Brownian motion (equivalently $\xi$ is Gaussian white noise).

In the context of physical or financial modelling, many other types of random inputs would make perfect sense, but Itô integrals are of no help. Countless books offer an introduction to Itô calculus without explicitly referring to this significant limitation. In such books, « Itô calculus » and « stochastic calculus » are used interchangeably, but Itô calculus has a very narrow scope.

We will rely on \cite{IW,KS,RY} and the seminal work \cite{Ito} [1] to recount a more accurate history. The advent of SDEs is to be found in the theory of Markov processes rather than in the theory of ODEs. My point is not to criticise authors who choose the latter approach to introduce SDEs. Their aim is to motivate the introduction of a material which has far exceeded its historical purpose.

A more accurate history of SDEs

It is well known that one can associate a homogeneous Markov process with an operator called the infinitesimal generator which describes how the process moves from point to point. For instance, if \(W_t\) is an \(\mathbb R^d\)-valued Brownian motion, \(X_t=x+b t+\sigma W_t\) is a Markov process with generator given by \begin{equation}\label{eq:generator_arithmetic_BM} Lf(x)=\sum_ib_i\partial_if(x)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\partial_{ij}^2f(x),\quad f\in\mathcal C^2_0\,, \end{equation} where \(a=\sigma\sigma^t\). More generally, a large class of Markov process with continuous sample paths can be shown to have the following generator \begin{equation}\label{eq:generator_general} Lf(x)=\sum_ib_i(x)\partial_i f(x)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}a_{ij}(x)\partial_{ij}^2f(x),\quad f\in\mathcal C^2_ K\,, \end{equation} where the matrix \(a(x)\) is symmetric and non-negative (see \cite{RY}, p. 267). These Markov processes are called diffusions. Let us suppose that there exists a Markov process \(X\) with generator \eqref{eq:generator_general}. Taking the generator at face value suggests that between times \(t\) and \(t+h\), \(X\) satisfies \begin{equation*} X_{t+h}=X_t+b(X_t)h+\sigma(X_t)(W_{t+h}-W_t)+o(h)\,, \end{equation*} where \(\sigma\sigma^t=a\) and \(W_t\) is a Brownian motion. One may express the same idea in integral form \begin{equation}\label{eq:SDE} X_t=x+\int_0^tb(X_s)\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(X_s)\,\dd W_s\,. \end{equation} Notice how \eqref{eq:SDE} resembles \eqref{eq:proto_SDE_integral}, even tough we obtained them in unrelated ways. The analytical problem we already mentioned still persists, but this time $W$ is naturally a Brownian motion.

This is how SDEs first appeared historically. People noticed that some Markov processes ought to satisfy stochastic differential equations. If one came up with a way of defining the (analytically challenging) second integral appearing in \eqref{eq:SDE}, then one could construct \(X\) as the solution to \eqref{eq:SDE}. According to \cite{IW,KS}, this approach to diffusions was proposed by P. Lévy and is dubbed in \cite{KS} as the methodology of stochastic differential equations [2]. The merit of defining the stochastic integral with respect to \(W\) rests on Itô and is presented in \cite{Ito}.

Analytical trouble, Young integrals and the \(p\)-variation

This section is about attempts to make sense of integrals with respect to $B$ relying on real analysis. That is to say, we are looking for an integration theory which extends Stieltjes integration while being general enough to allow integration with respect to typical realisations of Brownian motion.

Bearing in mind that we wish to make sense of integrals of the type $$ \int_0^tf_s\,\dd g_s\,, $$ we naturally wish to examine the convergence of Riemann-type sums \begin{equation}\label{eq:riemann_sums} \sum_i f_{t_i}(g_{t_{i+1}}-g_{t_i}) \end{equation} when the mesh of the partition goes to $0$. One may argue that Riemann sums are too simplistic, especially because we know that modern integration theory is based on the Lebesgue integral. That is true, but $B$ is not BV ($\dd B$ is not a measure), so there is no hope to interpret stochastic integrals as Lebesgue integrals with respect to a random measure.

Let us introduce $p$-variation, which is of particular interest when it comes to giving criteria for the convergence of Riemann sums.
Definition We define the \(p\)-variation on the interval \(I\subset[0,\infty)\) of any function \(f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb R\) as the quantity \begin{equation*} \|f\|_{p-\text{var}}^I:=\sup_{D(I)}\left(\sum_{k}(f(t_{k+1})-f(t_k))^p\right)^{1/p}\,, \end{equation*} where the supremum runs over all subdivisions of \(I\).

Remark \(2\)-variation is different from quadratic variation. For instance, $B$ famously has a finite quadratic variation, but we will soon see that its $2$-variation is infinite.

Here is the general criterion for the convergence of Riemann sums under $p$-variation hypotheses:

Theorem (Young-Kondurar) If $\|f\|_{p-\text{var}}^{[0,t]}<\infty$ and $\|g\|_{q-\text{var}}^{[0,t]}<\infty$ with $p^{-1}+q^{-1}>1$, then the Riemann-sums converge along any sequence of subvisions of $[0,t]$ with vanishing mesh, to the same limit. We call this unique limit the Young-Kondurar integral of $f$ w.r.t. $g$.

Remark Stieltjes integrals correspond to $q=1$ and any $p\ge1$.

The previous theorem is pretty significant, because the solution $x$ to \eqref{eq:proto_SDE_integral} is known to inherit its regularity from that of $g$. This means that, even assuming $\sigma$ to be smooth, we expect that $q=p$, and the criterion requires $p<2$. At this point, there is still hope that Young integrals are enoug to make sense of \eqref{eq:SDE}, but unfortunately:

Theorem For every \(p\le2\), for every interval \(I\subset[0,\infty)\), \(\|B\|_{p-\text{var}}^I=\infty\) a.s. In particular, a.s., \(B\) has infinite 2-variation over all intervals. The same result holds for any continuous semimartingale.

Quite often, courses on stochastic analysis insist that \(B\) has infinite variation (infinite \(1\)-variation) over all intervals, ruling out the possibility to use Stieltjes integrals to make sense of \eqref{eq:SDE}. The previous theorem rules out Young-Kondurar integrals altogether. This is a very significant analytical fact.

Remark En passant, we learned that controlled equations of the type \eqref{eq:ODE_controlled} can be made sense of by \eqref{eq:proto_SDE_integral} where the integral w.r.t. to $g$ is a Young integral, as long as $g$ has finite $p$-variation with $p<2$.

One might expect that $p$-variation is not the final answer, as there exist other ways to measure the regularity of functions, but here comes the final nail in the coffin.

Theorem (Lyons 91) There exists no separable Banach space $\mathcal B\subset\mathcal C([0,1])$ with the following properties

  • Samples paths of Brownian motion lie in $\mathcal B$ almost surely.
  • The map $(f,g)\mapsto\int_0^{\cdot} f_t\dot g_t\,\dd t$ defined on smooth functions extends to a continuous map from $\mathcal B\times\mathcal B$ into $\mathcal C([0,1])$.

Remark In particular, there exists no Banach topology on $\mathcal C([0,1])$ strong enough to keep the Sieltjes integral continuous in its arguments and weak enough to allow smooth paths to converge to Brownian paths. Lyons' theorem provides a quantitative answer to the claim that stochastic integration is analytically ill-posed by nature.

Roughness is scale-dependent

A natural way to interpret stochastic differential equations driven by Brownian motion is to approximate the driving noise by smooth processes (e.g. mollification or piecewise linear interpolation) and consider the corresponding classical differential equations. From a Physics perspective, this makes sense because white noise is an idealisation of rapidly varying smooth forces.
